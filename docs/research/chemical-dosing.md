# Chemical dosing and flow-paced control

- **Checked:** 2026-09-15
- **Question:** Which dosing control modes, ratio limits, permissives, actuation forms, totalization practices, failure behaviors, and alarm expectations do owners and regulators actually specify for water and wastewater chemical dosing, so that `WW-CTL-003` can carry observable acceptance criteria?
- **Affected requirements:** `WW-CTL-003`

## Supported findings

### Dosing control modes owners actually specify

- Flow-paced (ratio) control is the default owner requirement: chemical feed rates shall be proportional to flow, and a means to measure the water flow must be provided [1 §5.1.2]; UFGS directs proportional control for variable-flow systems and specifies a flow meter and controller that varies chemical dosage in proportion to measured flow with the dosage per flow unit manually adjustable [2 §2.2.4.1.1]; Sydney Water's Mode 2 runs dosing pumps at an operator-adjustable preset dose rate in mg/L against the measured sewer flow [7 §3.5]; hypochlorite at Clinton is flow-paced off the effluent flow meter [10]; GV Water requires flow-paced dosing [9].
- Fixed-rate control exists as a distinct declared mode: UFGS "manual control" — operator start/stop and feed-rate adjustment [2 §2.2.4.3]; Sydney Water's Mode 3 runs pumps at a fixed engineering-adjustable speed [7 §3.5]; vendor "manual" modes hold a set output [12, 13]. Constant-rate feeds are acceptable where the treated flow barely varies — CDC advises against flow-paced systems unless flow varies by more than 20% [3]; UFGS notes constant-rate systems should respond to pump operation or a flow switch instead [2 §2.2.4 note].
- Time-profiled dosing is a third specified form: Sydney Water's Mode 1 (diurnal) runs a 24-setpoint hourly profile, separate weekday/weekend profiles, with an operator multiplier and offset applied to the profile, bounded by min/max pump speed [7 §3.5].
- Feedback trim rides on top of flow pacing: the compound loop paces feed on flow and trims on a residual analyzer [4]; UFGS offers ORP or pH probe feedback varying feed rate against a setpoint deviation [2 §2.2.4.1.2–3]; GV Water requires residual control "where possible" with the required analyser [9]; phosphorus-removal practice distinguishes flow-proportional, load-proportional (flow × influent concentration), and feedback alternatives [17]. Feedback trim presumes an analyzer the dosing contract itself does not supply.
- Whether pacing is primary depends on variability: the sources converge on flow pacing as the norm where the treated flow varies, fixed-rate where it is steady, and analyzer trim where a residual must be held [1, 2, 3, 4, 7, 17]. Which single mode the first client calls primary is a validation question, but flow-paced is the mode owner specs require to exist.

### Ratio and setpoint limits — and behavior at the clamp

- The ratio setpoint is operator-adjustable in dose terms: "dosage per flow unit manually adjustable" [2 §2.2.4.1.1]; an operator-adjustable dose rate in mg/L [7]; a "pacing factor" or concentration input converting flow-proportional volume tasks into dose terms is vendor-standard [13].
- Rate limits are engineered bounds, not conventions: metering pumps should run near mid-range — 30–70% of capacity [3], most accurately near mid-range of stroke length and stroking frequency [5]; feeders shall not run below 20% of the feed range, or 10% when two independent adjustments are fitted [1 §5.0.3(g)]; pumps must meet the maximum rate against maximum head at the injection point [1 §5.1.4] with the maximum at least 10% above specified maximum flow [8]; ±2% accuracy over 100:1 turndown [2], ±1.5% repeatability over the turndown ratio [8]; stroke-frequency turndown 100:1 and stroke-length turndown 10:1 per actuator axis [16].
- At the clamp the demand saturates at the bound: Sydney Water bounds the computed pump speed by the engineering-adjustable minimum and maximum pump running speed [7]; vendor pumps expose a configurable speed limit [14]. Whether a clamped demand must alarm or merely flag is not stated in the owner specs — see assumptions.

### Permissives and interlocks

- Process-flow proof is the load-bearing permissive: the feed system must not operate unless water is being produced — metering pump wired in series with the well/service pump, or a flow switch for gravity flow, with a secondary flow-based device as backup for small systems [3]; feeders electrically interconnected with the well or service pump at automatically operated facilities [1 §5.1.2(b)]; a flow switch wired in series with the metering pump prevents overfeed during no-flow, and interlock to the well pump alone is not acceptable [6]; UFGS semiautomatic control starts/stops the metering equipment on flow switch, pump operation, or timer status, with the flow switch upstream of the injectors [2 §2.2.4.2].
- A permissive chain can be sequenced: at Sydney Water a call to run opens an automatic valve on the storage tank first — proven by limit switches — then starts the duty pump; loss of any dosing permissive stops the pump, closes the valve, and inhibits the system until another dosing-permissive signal [7].
- Tank-level inhibits are standard: dosing inhibited and alarmed on storage-tank low level, dosing-cabinet high level, or bund flood [7]; low-level and empty-tank signals stop or warn at the pump [12, 13].
- External inhibit conditions exist beyond the skid: a remote wet-weather interlock inhibits dosing [7]; tanker-unloading power outlets are interlocked to the storage-tank level transmitter and high-level switch [7].
- Anti-siphon/overfeed protection is a physical design constraint the logic should know about: siphon prevention by positive-pressure discharge, vacuum relief, or air gap [1 §5.1.5]; diaphragm antisiphon devices in the feed line [3]; a back-pressure valve where backpressure margin is insufficient to prevent flow-through at shutdown [2 §2.2.3 note].

### Actuation forms

- Speed command is the dominant actuation: variable-speed flow pacing [10]; an analog dose-rate or pump-speed setpoint over a hardwired interface, with speed locally and remotely adjustable while running [8]; 4–20 mA pump input and output required [9]; analog 0/4–20 mA control where dosed capacity is proportional to the input [12, 13]; 4–20 mA on peristaltic metering pumps [14, 15].
- Stroke frequency (pulses) is the second form: pulse modes dose a set volume per incoming pulse, e.g. from a contact water meter, with a transfer/scaling factor [12, 13]; external stroke-frequency control over 4–20 mA [16]; stroke-per-contact on/off stepping [13].
- Stroke length is a separate, usually manual or engineering, adjustment: manual stroke-length setting paired with variable-speed pacing [10]; stroke length locally and remotely adjustable while running only where a stroke servo is fitted [8]; electronic stroke length 0–100%, recommended 30–100% [13]; sizing guidance expects pumps to sit mid-range on both axes [3, 5].
- On/off metering pumps exist as remote-run/remote-stop digital commands (maintained preferred over pulsed) beside the analog setpoint — the PLC-side contract for simple skids [8]; a pacing relay can even step a second pump stroke-for-stroke [13].

### Totalization and consumption accounting

- Measuring consumption is an explicit standard requirement: provisions shall be made for measuring the quantities of chemicals used [1 §5.1.2(e)]; day tanks scale-mounted or carrying a calibrated gauge so unit readings relate meaningfully to the day's consumption [1 §5.1.11(d)].
- Consumption is cross-checked against delivered water: the master meter is read daily to confirm proper amounts fed [3]; Sydney Water confirms dosing by monitoring the storage-tank level trend over time [7].
- At the equipment level the check is a calibration: a calibration standpipe lets the operator time a measured drawdown at full stroke and maximum speed [2 §2.2.6]; vendor pumps self-measure actual dosing flow and emit it on an analog output [12] or count volume and revolutions internally [14]; per-stroke verification confirms each discharge [16].

### Failure behavior

- Loss of the pacing/flow signal is treated as a fault: a low-signal fault in analog mode that self-resets when the signal returns [15]; loss of the analog signal is a fault-relay condition [13]. The owner specs fix the safe direction indirectly — no flow means no feed [3, 6] — but do not state whether a bad flow signal should stop dosing, hold the last demand, or drop to a fallback rate; that is a declared-behavior question (assumptions).
- A proven no-discharge fault stops the pump: the flow monitor stops the pump after a set number of strokes without discharge and the fault relay alarms or activates a standby pump [13]; flow-verification faults alarm on a dry contact and optionally shut the pump down [16]; pump stroke alone does not prove delivery — a ruptured diaphragm still strokes — which is why the discharge line needs flow proof [13, 16].
- Standby redundancy is required where feed is essential: a minimum of two feeders for processes protecting the supply, with the standby able to replace the largest unit [1 §5.1.1]; duty/standby pumps online at primary disinfection sites [9]; duty/standby dosing pumps with sequenced valve-open-then-start [7].
- Recovery expectations: remote restart after power interruption [8, 14]; a flow-verification fault is remotely resettable only after physical checks clear the cause [15].

### Alarm set

- Owner-specified skid alarms: pump running, pump fault (no flow/overload), tank level low, bund flood [9]; dilution-water low flow, batching-tank level HH/LL/no-change, transfer-pump no-flow, storage-tank low-level, dosing-cabinet high-level, bund flood, wet-weather inhibit [7].
- Vendor fault set adds: loss of analog/pacing signal [13, 15], no-discharge/loss-of-flow [13, 16], diaphragm rupture [13], pressure min/max [12], empty tank distinct from low level [12, 13], drive fault and leak detect [14, 15].
- The overfeed direction has its own alarm/event category: overfeed events carry defined emergency procedures [3]; the no-flow interlocks above exist precisely to prevent overfeed [3, 6].
- Chemical-specific hazards produce site alarms outside the pump contract: bund flood [7, 9], gas detection, tank high-level during filling [7].

### Manual and local takeover

- Manual override of automatic control is a standard requirement [1 §1.1.11, §5.1.2(a)]; UFGS defines the modes as automatic, semiautomatic (auto start/stop on a condition), and manual (operator start/stop and rate adjustment) [2 §2.2.4].
- Local/remote is a declared interface state: hardwired local- and remote-selected indications beside the remote-run/remote-stop/analog-setpoint contract [8]; local versus remote control selection with remote stop displayed [15]; local HMI modes (manual/pulse/analog/batch/timer) coexist with remote analog or fieldbus control [12, 13, 14].
- Manual dosing practice is operator-set speed plus operator-set stroke at the pump [10]; remote write protection (parameter locking) is expected where a communications interface exists, and comms never replace the hardwired control signals [8].

## Existing seams versus contract gaps

What the current component kinds, descriptors, and typed-build seam already express:

- Feedback trim: `pid` plus `analog-input`/`signal-filter` express the residual/analyzer loop [4, 17]; `rate-limiter` bounds demand slew.
- Permissives and interlocks: `interlock` (permissive plus trip inputs driving a safe value), `bool-gate`, `sr-latch`, and debounced `digital-input` cover flow-switch-proven, tank-level, and bund/cabinet inhibits [1, 3, 6, 7]; `sequencer` covers the valve-first run sequence [7].
- Actuation: `motor` covers the on/off run command with failed-feedback diagnostics and `analog-output` carries a speed demand [8, 10]; `counter` counts strokes for stroke-based accounting [16].
- Operator surface: `manual-station` gives bumpless manual takeover of an analog demand [1 §5.1.2(a), 10]; writable internal `In` points and `set_parameter` carry the dose setpoint and tuning [2 §2.2.4.1.1, 7]; `override-select` chooses between demand sources.
- Accounting: `totalizer` integrates a Float rate — commanded dose rate or measured chemical flow [1 §5.1.2(e), 3].
- Measurement and quality: `analog-input` carries the pacing flow; quality propagation marks a bad flow signal; `failover-select` switches to a declared backup source on quality [13, 15].
- Alarms: `alarm-monitor` and `latching-alarm` compose the skid set (low/empty tank, no flow, bund, wet-weather inhibit, pump fault) [7, 9].
- Group coordination: `pump-group` manages a duty/standby pair — rotation, availability, demand staging — reusable for duty/standby dosing pumps [1 §5.1.1, 7, 9].

What they cannot express — the gap list WW-CTL-003 implementation tickets would consume:

1. **Flow-paced ratio demand.** No kind computes "dose per flow unit × measured flow → actuator demand" with declared dose and rate limits, a clamped/saturated indication, and a declared response to a bad flow signal (stop / hold last / fallback rate). Composing it from `pid` misuses the controller; from `bool-gate`s it has no semantics. → a ratio/flow-pacing component kind with `flow`, optional `trim`, `demand`, `clamped`, and quality-driven fallback parameters.
2. **Trim composition.** The compound loop needs the analyzer `pid` to *scale* the paced demand (multiplicative trim or a trimmed dose setpoint [4]) — a port-wired cascade, so the ratio kind needs a declared trim input rather than a new trim contract.
3. **Dose-account verification.** "Confirmation of dosing" compares commanded demand integrated over time against measured consumption (tank drawdown trend, discharge flow, or stroke count) [3, 7, 12, 16]. `totalizer` + `alarm-monitor` can compare totals only if a measured-consumption signal exists; expected-vs-measured deviation semantics want a declared monitor — → a consumption-verification composition or small deviation kind.
4. **Metering-pump actuation contract.** Two-axis command (speed demand plus stroke setting) and run-command-only skids are both specified [8, 10, 16]; whether the library models a metering-pump device kind (run Bool + speed Float + fault in) or stays at `motor` + `analog-output` composition is a `dcs-build` assembly decision.
5. **Time-profiled (diurnal) dosing.** Sydney Water's 24-setpoint hourly profile [7] needs a time-of-day source; components own no wall clock (decision 4), so a diurnal mode needs either a schedule kind fed by an external tick-mapped time input or an explicitly out-of-scope deferral.
6. **Skid-level declared fallback states.** "No flow → which safe state" and "analyzer bad → paced-only" are failure-driven mode switches like the station's `failover-select` but for a demand path; composing per skid re-implements policy. → declared `on_bad_flow`/`on_bad_trim` parameters on the ratio kind (same pattern as `threshold-chain.on_bad_demand`).

## Proposed DCS implications

*All items in this section are proposals, not decided behavior.*

- Model the dosing function as one declared component — a ratio/flow-pacing kind — consuming the measured process flow and an operator dose setpoint (per flow unit, e.g. mg/L), emitting the actuator demand bounded by declared min/max dose and rate limits with a `clamped` flag, and a declared `on_bad_flow` response (stop, hold, or fallback fixed rate) alarmed on transition. Flow pacing is the default mode the sources require [1, 2, 7, 9, 10]; fixed-rate is the fallback mode and a valid primary only where flow is steady [3, 6].
- Carry an optional `trim` input so a residual/quality `pid` can scale the paced demand for the compound-loop form [4, 9, 17]; declare `on_bad_trim` behavior (pace on flow alone) rather than letting trim failure propagate silently.
- Treat permissives as an interlock chain the dosing demand passes through: process flow proven (flow above a minimum / pump operation / flow switch), metering pump available, tank level above the inhibit, no external inhibit (wet weather, bund flood) [3, 6, 7]; loss of any permissive drops demand to the safe value and inhibits restart until permissives return [7].
- Represent the metering pump as run-command plus speed-demand actuation, with stroke length an engineering/operator setting rather than a control output [8, 10]; expose the pump's proven-fault path (no-discharge detection, drive fault) as a failed-feedback or dedicated fault input that alarms and can hand over to the standby pump [13, 16].
- Totalize consumption from the commanded or measured chemical rate [1 §5.1.2(e)] and, where a measured-consumption signal exists (tank drawdown, discharge flow, stroke count), compose a dose-confirmed deviation check [3, 7, 12, 16].
- Compose the skid alarm set from `latching-alarm` instances — pump running/fault, tank low and empty, loss of pacing signal, no discharge, bund flood, external inhibit, dose not confirmed — consistent with owner lists [7, 9] and the vendor fault set [12–16].
- Keep manual/local takeover as `manual-station` on the demand plus the equipment's local/remote indication [8, 10, 15]; declare which protections persist in manual, matching the station pattern.

## Assumptions needing customer validation

- Which control mode is primary for the first client: flow-paced ratio (the owner-specified norm), fixed-rate (justified only where treated flow varies little [3]), or diurnal profile [7]; and whether analyzer trim is in scope for the first deployment [4, 9, 17].
- The declared response to a bad pacing-flow signal — stop dosing (the conservative reading of the no-flow interlocks [3, 6]), hold last demand, or a fallback fixed rate — and whether it must be operator-selectable.
- Whether a clamped demand only flags or also alarms, and which limits are operator-tunable versus engineering-fixed [3, 7, 8].
- The actuation contract per pump: speed-only, speed-plus-stroke-servo, or run-only on/off [8, 10, 16]; and whether pulse-per-stroke (water-meter contact) pacing must be an input form [12, 13].
- The required skid alarm set and which alarms latch until acknowledged; whether bund/cabinet/environmental alarms live in the dosing contract or the station's [7, 9].
- Whether dose confirmation is integration-of-command only or requires a measured-consumption signal (tank drawdown, discharge flowmeter, stroke count) [3, 7, 12, 16].
- Manual-takeover scope — remote manual over the operator path, pump-local control only, or both — and which permissives persist in manual [1 §5.1.2(a), 8, 15].
- Whether chemical-tank inventory management (day-tank limits, fill interlocks, delivery logging [1 §5.1.11, 7]) is part of the dosing component or a separate tank component.
