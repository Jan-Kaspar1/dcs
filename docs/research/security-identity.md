# Operator identity, authorization, audit retention, and security-zone requirements for the water-sector DCS

- **Checked:** 2026-09-15
- **Question:** What do water-sector owner specifications, standards, and regulator or sector guidance actually require of operator identities and role-based authorization on a DCS/SCADA — who may write points, tune parameters, force inputs, acknowledge alarms, and promote controllers; what authentication is expected at unattended stations and on remote access paths; what the audit record must contain and how long it must live; what security-zone and conduit partitioning is expected where owner documents cite it; and where the control system's own obligations end versus site and network infrastructure — so that `WW-SEC-001` can carry either observable acceptance criteria or a concrete revisit condition?
- **Affected requirements:** `WW-SEC-001`; edges touch `WW-OPS-002` (alarm acknowledgment attribution), `WW-LCM-001`/`WW-LCM-002` (promotion authority and journal retention — the lifecycle note's audit findings in `docs/research/deployment-commissioning.md` are reused here)

## Supported findings

### Who may command — owner-specified roles and privilege levels

- Role-tiered authorization on the operator surface is owner-specified, not just standards-aspirational. The owner documents converge on a small ladder of named privilege levels: six user groups against a named permission matrix — Read, Browse, Control, Override/Release, Exclusive Control, Manage Exclusive Control, System Admin — in one regional utility standard [21]; three access levels with enumerated rights — Operator (view, modify *some* setpoints, acknowledge alarms, control equipment), Supervisor (all setpoints, totalizer resets, user administration), Developer (unrestricted, for development only) — in a municipal SCADA procurement [23]; three application-wide role logins in a city HMI standard [16]; and a security-privilege-level scheme referenced by a state utility's design standard, where differing authority levels for setpoint changes are required outright [18 §12.2.1; 19].
- Which *actions* map to which level is engineering data, not convention: the municipal procurement requires a control document identifying the access level of each setpoint and control [23]; the utility standard separates ordinary process/control setpoints from critical-control-point parameters, and keeps a class of parameter — PLC parameters — off the operator interface entirely, hard-coded in the controller [19]. Alarm-side authority is specified the same way: per-alarm owners and acknowledgement authority are alarm-design data [19], and shelving/disabling alarms is gated by the user-role scheme [20].
- The standards supply the normative frame: authorization enforcement for all users, with permission mapping to roles, scaling to supervisor override and dual approval at the higher security levels [1 SR 2.1 and RE 1–4], under least privilege as a stated common constraint [1 §4.4; 13; 25].
- Honest counter-evidence on identity granularity: one city HMI standard uses *shared* role logins — every operator logs in as "Operator" — with per-function numeric access levels doing the authorization work [16]. Sector guidance and regulation push the other way — unique individual credentials [5 2.C; 10 §5-E.6(c)(2)(iii),(v); 22; 25 §3.8.3] — and the New York rule expressly forces group credentials to be changed or compensated [10]. Whether a deployment authenticates individuals or workstations' roles is therefore an owner-policy variable the platform cannot fix in advance.
- No gathered source names promotion of a redundant controller as a separately authorized action; the high-consequence categories the sources do name — overrides/exclusive control [21], unrestricted developer rights [23], administrator privileges [16, 22], CCP parameters [19] — are where controller-switch authority would sit if an owner classed it.

### Authentication — unattended stations and remote access paths

- The standards baseline is unique identification and authentication of all human users, with multifactor authentication required for access from untrusted networks at the third security level and for all networks at the fourth [1 SR 1.1 and RE 1–3]; account, identifier, and authenticator management are system-level capabilities in the same family [1 SR 1.3–1.5].
- Sector guidance and regulation set the remote-access floor concretely: MFA at minimum for remote access to the OT network, including vendor and maintenance accounts and HMI applications [6 factsheet 2.H; 5 2.H; 13]; by regulation, MFA for any external-network access to a water system's OT unless compensating controls are approved in writing, remote access disabled unless necessary to monitor or operate the system, remote-access functionality limited to necessary functions, and no default passwords in OT [10 §5-E.6(c)(2)]. The joint fact sheet records why: exposed HMIs were exploited to max out setpoints, disable alarms, and lock out operators — the exact seam between authentication and operator authority [8].
- Owner remote-access practice is infrastructure-shaped: a secure VPN into the station router plus password-protected remote-viewing software for operators, vendors, and contractors [23]; Active Directory as the authentication authority with optional smart/proximity cards [22]; centralized account management in a domain or the HMI's own provider [16, 22]. Authentication machinery — directory, VPN, MFA service — lives in site infrastructure in every owner document gathered; none expects the control product to be its own identity authority.
- Unattended-station expectation evidence is thinner and indirect: automatic session logout after a defined inactivity period appears in one owner standard [22], and the standards name session lock, remote session termination, concurrent-session control, unsuccessful-login-attempt handling, and system-use notification as capabilities [1 SR 1.11–1.12, 2.5–2.7]. For a station whose normal state is unstaffed with alarms delivered to a staffed facility (the Ten States Standards pattern cited in `docs/research/pumping-station.md`), the sources point at exposure minimization and remote-access control [6 1.A; 8; 13 #3] rather than at in-product login machinery on the station itself.
- Owners also require custody of access: integrator-side credential locks on delivered control logic are prohibited or must be handed to the owner [17; 24]. The platform withholding credentials from the owner is a named anti-pattern in owner documents.

### Audit-record content and retention — what the journaled record feeds

- The standards define the audit surface: the control system must generate audit records for defined auditable events (with a centrally managed, system-wide audit trail as the level-2 enhancement), manage audit storage capacity with a threshold warning, define the response to audit-processing failure, timestamp records, and support non-repudiation at higher levels [1 SR 2.8–2.12]; separately, authorized read access to audit logs served as automatically as possible through programming interfaces, plus continuous monitoring [1 SR 6.1–6.2].
- Owner documents name the content an operator-action record carries: a required "Operator Event Log" is a SCADA-software deliverable in the utility design standard [18 §12.5.1]; alarm presentation is specified field-by-field — alarm identity, date, time, and acknowledgment status (per the county standard cited in `docs/research/deployment-commissioning.md`) — and procurement expects access levels recorded per setpoint and control [23], which presumes the log binds actor to action to target.
- Sector guidance frames the security log's purpose — detection and investigation — and its protection: collect logs of system and network access, and where an OT asset cannot log, collect the network traffic to and from it [6 2.T]; protect logs from unauthorized access and tampering [6 2.U]; sufficient logging is baseline [7]; long-term retrievability of records is an explicit municipal-policy obligation [25 §3.7.4]; the New York rule requires larger water systems to monitor and log network activity and be prepared to produce the logs for incident investigation [10 §5-E.6(c)(6)]; the UK framework assesses security monitoring capability and periodic access-list review against the sector annually [11 C1, B2.a; 12].
- Retention periods are fixed only for the *compliance monitoring record*: NPDES monitoring information at least 3 years (5+ for sludge) and drinking-water analysis/corrective-action records 3–10 years [15] — the journaled and historized record is the feedstock those are produced from, per the lifecycle note. No gathered source fixes a retention period for the control system's own security/operator audit log; the expectations found are investigatory availability, tamper protection, and retrievability across format changes [1 SR 6.1; 6 2.T–2.U; 10; 25 §3.7.4]. The retention number itself is an owner-policy fact.

### Security zones and conduits — where the standard's language lands in owner documents

- The zoning process is standardized: partition the system under consideration into zones and conduits — expressly separating business assets from IACS assets, safety-related assets, temporarily connected devices, wireless devices, and devices connected via external networks — then assess risk and assign each zone and conduit a target security level, and document the security requirements [2 scope, ZCR 3.1–3.6, ZCR 5.6]. The system-side requirements the zoning feeds are network segmentation, zone-boundary protection, and application partitioning [1 SR 5.1–5.4]; the equivalent segmentation guidance appears in the US OT guide's architecture model [4 §5.3–5.4].
- Owner documents adopt the vocabulary at architecture level rather than point level: a utility design standard has a network-security section and defines DMZ as a network security zone among its required design deliverables [18 §16.7]; a city SCADA manual carries cyber and network-architecture appendices [22]; a municipal PLC standard dedicates a section to control-network design across the plant (per the lifecycle note). The vendor blueprint shows the shape applied to water plants — facility zones, a DMZ perimeter, remote stations as separate zones [26] — consistent with the joint fact sheet's DMZ/bastion recommendation at the OT boundary [8].
- Two consequences fall out of the standard's own structure: the SL-T is assigned by the owner's risk process per zone, so "which security level must the product meet" has no single answer — it is per-zone deployment data [2]; and the product's zoning obligation reduces to exposing clean partition points — the interfaces across which a conduit boundary can be drawn — not owning the boundaries.

### Where the control system's obligations end versus site and network infrastructure

- The standards split responsibilities by design: system requirements are phrased as capabilities the control system *provides* ("the control system shall provide the capability to…" [1]); the security *program* — account lifecycle, periodic access review, policies, compensating measures where a system lacks a capability — is the asset owner's, and the owner-program part states outright that it does not require the IACS itself to have the technical requirements [3; 11 B2's organizational measures sit on the same side].
- Regulation binds the operating organization, not the product: the RRA obligation sits on the community water system [9]; the New York program requirements on the covered water system [10]; NIS duties on the water company as Operator of Essential Services [12]. A product's role in those obligations is to supply the capabilities and records the owner's program consumes.
- The owner documents instantiate the split concretely: authentication machinery is Active Directory or the HMI's security provider [16, 22]; remote access is the owner's VPN/router path [23]; account and user-database administration is a Supervisor/Developer privilege inside the owner's organization [23]; and the owner requires custody of all credentials over its own logic [17, 24]. The platform's share ends at carrying verified identity through its actions, bounding the command surface, and producing the audit record — the boundary decision 48 already records.

## Existing seams versus contract gaps

What the implemented mechanisms already cover, needing no contract work:

- **The who/what/when operator-action record.** Every settled command — writes, parameter tunes, forces/unforces — journals as `CommandSettled` carrying the receipt with its declared `actor`, in tick order (decisions 7, 17, 39; #20, #35, #165). With `--journal-file` the record is durable across restarts, append-only, opens each process lifetime with a run-boundary marker, and treats an append failure as fatal rather than a silent gap (decision 36; #163) — the audit-trail shape the standards' auditable-events and audit-failure-response requirements describe [1 SR 2.8, 2.10] and the owner operator-event-log expectation [18 §12.5.1].
- **Programmatic audit access.** `GET /journal` serves the record with since-cursors — the automatically-served read access SR 6.1 describes at interface level [1]; authentication of that access is the fronting seam's, below.
- **A declared, enforced command surface.** `writable` is model-declared per point, validated, and enforced at submission with `CommandError::NotWritable`; `Out` points can never be command targets (decision 18; #49). This is the same shape as the owner pattern of keeping a parameter class off the operator interface entirely [19] and recording an access level per setpoint [23] — the platform's version declares the *commandable* surface as engineering data, one step below per-role grading.
- **Role and lifecycle transitions are journaled events.** `RoleChanged`, divergence verdicts, reinitializations, quality transitions, and step failures all journal (decisions 19, 25–26; #46, #87) — the state-change audit content, alongside the commands.
- **The declared-actor fronting seam.** `POST /command` accepts the attributed envelope and a fronting authenticating proxy can fill `actor` from authenticated context with no contract change (decisions 39, 48; #165) — matching the owner pattern where authentication machinery is the site's directory/VPN layer [16, 22, 23] and the product-side share is carrying verified identity into the journaled record.
- **Nothing blocks the owner's custody expectations.** Model documents are deployment-supplied files the owner holds (decision 46), and the journal file is a plain line-delimited record — the no-lockout posture owner standards require [17, 24] is intrinsic to the artifact set.
- **Bounded state surfaces for zoning.** Every externally reachable path is one of three documented transports — the monitor's HTTP surface, the peer checkpoint link, and the remote-driver link (decisions 8, 12; `docs/packaging.md`) — so a deployment's conduit boundaries have named places to sit [2; 8; 26].

What would need contract work or a recorded decision — the gap list implementation tickets would consume:

1. **Per-role authorization on the command surface.** `writable` is binary per point: nothing distinguishes an operator-setpoint write from an override-class write, a `set_parameter` tune, a force, or an ack — the graded permission matrix the owner standards specify [16, 19, 21, 23; 1 SR 2.1]. Options and their trade: declaring a required privilege tier beside `writable` (model data the fronting proxy enforces — extends decision 18's pattern without in-product accounts) versus in-contract role enforcement (reopens decision 48 outright).
2. **Unattributed switch requests.** `promote`/`demote` carry no actor field — decision 39 records it — so the highest-consequence operator action is the one the journal cannot attribute. Attribution is a small envelope extension; whether promotion must additionally be role-gated or dual-approved [1 SR 2.1 RE 3–4] is the policy question on top.
3. **Managed-state and lifecycle authority.** Alarm acknowledgment rides the ordinary writable `ack` point and shelving does not yet exist as a kind (decision 38) — so "who may acknowledge" and "who may disable an alarm" [19, 20] have no authorization answer until roles exist (gap 1). Same shape for forcing (decision 21).
4. **Journal retention, export, and integrity.** The journal file is durable and append-only but carries no declared retention, rotation/archive mechanism, or tamper-evidence; the lifecycle note recorded the same gap. The sources fix investigatory availability, log protection, and retrievability [1 SR 2.9, 6.1; 6 2.T–2.U; 10; 25 §3.7.4] rather than a number — so the deliverable is a declared retention/export convention plus, if a client specifies it, an integrity mechanism; the regulatory periods [15] bind the records produced *from* the journal.
5. **Read-side access control.** Every monitor GET is unauthenticated today; owner matrices grade read/browse too [21]. If a client requires in-product read restriction — versus the proxy denying unauthenticated reads — that is contract surface.
6. **Session semantics.** Session lock, inactivity logout, remote-session termination, and concurrent-session limits [1 SR 2.5–2.7; 22] presuppose sessions the stateless HTTP surface does not have; the evidence so far places them at the HMI/OS layer [22], not the controller — a recorded-position question.
7. **Access-denied visibility.** Unsuccessful-login handling and failed-attempt tracking [1 SR 1.11; 8] apply to the authenticating layer — currently the proxy, whose logs are outside the journal. Whether denied commands should journal inside the product is a contract question only if enforcement moves in-product.
8. **Zone/conduit documentation.** The product's conduit map is implicit in `docs/packaging.md` rather than a declared artifact; an owner zoning deliverable [18 §16.7] would consume a documented boundary list — documentation work, not contract work.

## Proposed DCS implications

*All items in this section are proposals, not decided behavior.*

- Keep enforcement at the decision-48 seam and sharpen what the contract guarantees: every externally submitted action carries `actor`; every settled action journals; the model declares the commandable surface. The owner's identity stack (directory, MFA, VPN) authenticates and authorizes [3, 16, 22, 23]; the product makes those decisions *effective* and *auditable* rather than making them.
- If a client's specification requires the product to know roles, prefer declaring a required privilege tier on `writable` points and component parameters — model data the proxy enforces and the UI dims — over in-contract accounts. That extends decision 18's declaration pattern to the graded permission matrices the owner documents specify [16, 21, 23] without moving identity storage into the controller.
- Extend the attributed envelope to `promote`/`demote` (gap 2) so every journaled action class carries a declared actor; whether switch authority needs gating beyond attribution is the same client question as gap 1.
- Record journal handling as deployment convention rather than machinery: a rotation/archival procedure over the append-only file feeds the availability and retrievability expectations [1 SR 6.1; 6 2.T–2.U; 25 §3.7.4], and tamper-evidence stays an open question until a client audit-integrity specification reopens decision 48.
- Document the product's conduit set — monitor surface, peer link, remote-driver link — as a named boundary list in the packaging documentation, giving a zone designer [2; 18 §16.7; 26] the partition points without the product claiming to own zoning.

## Assumptions needing customer validation

- The client's role model: a named ladder (operator/supervisor/engineer/administrator [16, 21, 23]), per-point numeric access levels [16], or directory-group mapping [22] — and which action classes (setpoint writes, tuning, forces, acknowledgment, promotion) map to which tier.
- Whether authorization must be enforced inside the product or is satisfied by an authenticating fronting proxy against the site's directory — the decision-48 reopening condition. Owner documents place authentication in site infrastructure [16, 22, 23]; nothing yet says the controller itself must decide.
- Whether promotion/demotion, forcing, or alarm acknowledgment must be role-gated, dual-approved, or merely attributed [1 SR 2.1 RE 3–4].
- The required retention period and integrity posture for the operator-action journal — availability and protection are specified [6 2.T–2.U; 10; 25 §3.7.4]; a number and a tamper-evidence requirement are not.
- Whether unattended-station interfaces need in-product session lock/inactivity logout [1 SR 2.5–2.7; 22], or whether the station surface stays exposure-minimized with session policy at the OS/HMI layer.
- The account-granularity policy: unique individual credentials [5 2.C; 10; 25] versus shared role logins [16] — and, if shared, what attribution the `actor` string carries.
- Whether the client assigns IEC 62443 target security levels per zone [2], and if so which SL-T the controller's zone must meet — that answer decides how much of FR 1/FR 2 is the product's versus compensating measures [3].
