## Public failure example — IJmuiden, 2 November 2023

During a rising tide, a fault at the IJmuiden discharge and pumping complex switched the discharge gates to manual operation and they did not close. The official evaluation says operators at the remote Schellingwoude operating position could not notice the open gates soon enough because they lacked sufficient visibility of the fault messages, gate positions, and rising canal level [18, 19]. Coordinated intervention prevented the Amsterdam level from becoming problematic; the exact initiating technical cause had not been established when Rijkswaterstaat published the evaluation [18, 19].

Rijkswaterstaat's corrective measures are direct alarm-design evidence: it moved operation to the site, placed a contractor there continuously, added a separate acoustic and visual signal for rising canal level, and retrained operators and maintenance staff [19]. ILT separately identified the increased high-water safety risk and required recurrence-prevention measures [20]. The user-supplied [NL Times account](https://nltimes.nl/2024/09/03/tech-failure-nearly-caused-massive-flood-amsterdam-city-center-november-2023) is retained as the public-facing example; the requirements rely on the official evaluations.

The product implication is broader than a high-level alarm. The operator must see a consequential transition away from automatic control, confirmed final-element position, process trend and rate of change, signal quality, and the causal order in one workflow. Acknowledgment records awareness but cannot make the active hazard disappear. Where hazard analysis requires automatic action, the alarm-and-operator path is not itself the protective function [21].

## Supported findings

### The lifecycle and state model the standards define

- An alarm is an audible and/or visible indication to the operator of an equipment malfunction, process deviation, or abnormal condition *requiring a response* [1 §3]. The operator-response requirement is the classification boundary: indications needing no operator response are events or alerts, not alarms — rationalization exists to enforce it [4, 7].
- The standard organizes alarm work as a lifecycle — philosophy, identification, rationalization, basic and detailed design, implementation, operation, maintenance, monitoring and assessment, management of change, and audit [1 §5.2; 2 §5.2]. The managed states below are lifecycle outputs, not just runtime flags: shelving rules, approved advanced techniques, and testing are alarm-philosophy content [2 §6.2], and detecting suppression applied outside proper work practices is a monitoring-stage duty [7].
- The state model distinguishes process truth from operator awareness: normal, unacknowledged, acknowledged, and returned-to-normal-unacknowledged (the condition cleared before acknowledgment, still requiring an ack to reset), with an optional latched behavior holding the indication until acknowledged even after return to normal [1 §5.3, Figure 3; 7]. Orthogonal to these, three managed states — shelved, suppressed-by-design, and out-of-service — may be entered from and returned to any alarm state [1 §5.3.2.6–5.3.2.8; 2 §5.3].
- The response timeline formalizes the quantities rationalization uses: the allowable response time before a consequence becomes unavoidable, the consequence threshold, and the alarm deadband [2 §5.4]. The alarm system scope includes the alarm and event log, the alarm historian, and performance-metric generation [2 scope]; rationalization records each alarm's consequence, required action, response time, priority, and class in a master alarm database [4]. IEC 62682 additionally names *highly managed alarms* — alarms carrying stricter training, testing, and record requirements [2 §§13–15].

### Shelving — temporary, bounded, tracked removal

- Shelving is operator-initiated temporary suppression for a limited period of time, the mechanism for nuisance alarms or alarms temporarily invalid — known-broken equipment, calibration, equipment down for maintenance [1 §11.7; 12; 15]. The operator who shelves remains accountable; shelved alarms are reviewed and justified at shift transition [15], and operational shelving carries record requirements including the shelving reason [2 §14.3; 17].
- The bounds are per-alarm engineering data: each alarm is pre-assigned a maximum shelving time, including zero for alarms that may never be shelved [15]. Return is automatic on expiry — or manual via unshelve — and a shelved-alarm list keeps the state visible [15, 17; 2 §11.7.4].

### Suppression — by design, by state, and out-of-service

- Suppressed-by-design is intentional non-annunciation under a designed condition — a category spanning simple logic-based gating to state-based alarming, not a single mechanism [1 §11.9; 7]. The suppressed condition is still required to be visible: suppressed-by-design alarms get their own display treatment [2 §11.9.4].
- State-based alarming is the designed-suppression family keyed to operating state: alarms suppressed while equipment is stopped or offline, alarm attributes (setpoint, priority) changed by state, first-out alarming that isolates the initiating cause, and flood suppression around a complex equipment trip [5 §6]. The trigger is plant state, so the suppression is engineered and reviewable rather than an operator action.
- Out-of-service is the maintenance-associated managed state: the alarm is non-functioning, applied and removed manually — unlike shelving there is no automatic return — and accountability sits with maintenance rather than the operator [1 §11.8; 15; 2 §§11.8, 15.3]. Visibility obligations parallel shelving: tracked status, dedicated display, and a defined return-to-service process [2 §§11.8.3, 15.3.5].

### Priority, classification, and rationalization

- Priority is assigned in rationalization from two factors — the severity of the consequence of inaction and the time the operator has to respond — through the alarm philosophy's prioritization matrix, typically producing three or four levels [4 Table 1; 8]. Priority is presentation-relevant data: the standards specify priority indication requirements including colour [2 §11.4], and performance targets expect an annunciated distribution near 80/15/5 low/medium/high [16].
- Class is an administrative grouping orthogonal to priority, assigned in rationalization for handling rules (for example which alarms are highly managed) [4]. Rationalization is also where alarms die: a candidate with no consequence of inaction, no required operator action, or no time to act is not configured [4].
- Owner evidence exists at program scale: a municipal water department publishes a full ISA-18.2-based alarm standard with prioritization and per-equipment minimum-alarm tables [8], and an owner control narrative marks alarm criticality per alarm [9]. Practitioner guidance reduces this to the water-sector minimum — a documented alarm database, gathered statistics, and periodic review [13].

### Alarm flood and performance monitoring

- A flood is defined quantitatively: an alarm rate greater than the operator can effectively manage, conventionally more than 10 alarms per 10 minutes per operator position [1 §16; 16]. Recommended targets bound average rates (≈150/day acceptable, ≈300/day maximum manageable; ≈1–2 per 10 minutes), the fraction of 10-minute periods above 10 alarms (<1%), chattering and fleeting alarms (zero), and stale alarms standing more than 24 hours [16; 6].
- Flood handling is designed, not improvised: state-based flood suppression around equipment trips [5 §6.7], elimination of chattering sources through deadbands and on/off delays [12, 15], shelving of known nuisance alarms [15], and first-out alarming to isolate the initiating cause [5 §6.2]. Detection and verification require the monitoring machinery — the alarm log and the metrics over it [2 §16; 6].
- Water-sector operating evidence confirms floods are a real problem in the target market, not only a refinery concern: a distributed municipal 3-waters network's recurrent floods produced documented operator alarm fatigue and cognitive overload [14], and sector practitioners name nuisance alarms a growing water problem while noting deployed water SCADA often lacks shelving, priorities, conditional alarming, and deadbands [12].

### What the owner specifications add — and what they do not

- Owner specs for the reference station class specify the *alarm set* and its *delivery*, not managed states: the regulated minimum alarm list on a backed-up supply, transmitted to a 24-hour staffed facility or responsible personnel with local audio-visual annunciation [11]; per-pump and station fault lists [10]; criticality marking per alarm [9]. Alarm routing and escalation — re-alarming and escalation paths — are likewise a standards-defined concern [5 §8] that maps to the telemetry obligation, not to lifecycle state.
- One owner datapoint answers part of the latch question: a latched high-level alarm requiring operator reset is specified in a utility fault list [10]. Which *other* alarms must latch until acknowledged remains unanswered by the sources — it is per-alarm customer policy.
- None of the small-station owner specifications gathered for the reference plant require shelving, out-of-service handling, or flood suppression for a duplex lift station; the demand evidence for the full lifecycle sits at owner-program scale (a utility-wide alarm standard [8], a citywide network's flood experience [14]), not at station scale.

## Existing seams versus contract gaps

What the implemented model and the decision-38 per-kind extension seam already express, needing no contract work:

- The acknowledgment axis in full. `latching-alarm` and `bool-latching-alarm` carry `alarm` (standing process truth) plus `unacknowledged` (latched on trip, cleared while the writable `ack` point reads true): normal, unacknowledged, acknowledged, and returned-to-normal-unacknowledged all fall out of the two flags — a condition clearing before ack leaves `alarm` false and `unacknowledged` held, which is the standards' RTN-unacknowledged state. The journaled settled receipt is the acknowledgment audit, and the latch rides checkpoints.
- Per-alarm latch policy as model data. Whether an alarm holds an indication until acknowledged — the owner-specified latched reset [10] — is the per-instance kind choice (`latching-alarm`/`bool-latching-alarm` versus the auto-clearing `alarm-monitor`), an engineering declaration, not a contract question. The standards' optional latched sub-state [1 §5.3] maps onto the same choice.
- Suppression-by-design and by state as wiring. Decision 38's recorded answer: gating an alarm's `in` port through `interlock`/`bool-gate` driven by equipment state is designed suppression [5 §6.3] — and the station kinds already expose the gating sources (`pump-group` `avail_i`/`fault_i`/`none_available`, `failover-select` `backup_active`, mode points). State-driven suppression composes today; what wiring cannot yet do is *name* the suppressed state to the operator (below).
- Priority and class as declared metadata. Decision 38 names the route: a declared parameter or descriptor metadata on an alarm kind — matching how owner specs attach criticality per alarm [9] — rendered by the descriptor-driven pane without a contract change.
- Shelving as a per-kind sibling. A shelving kind holding shelved-for ticks, with shelve/unshelve as writable points on the same receipted path as `ack`, is the same machinery #109 used — automatic return is a timer in kind state, the per-alarm maximum shelve time a declared parameter [15].
- Performance metrics as computed data. Every alarm transition already lands in point history and the durable journal, and acknowledgments in settled receipts — activation rates, flood periods, stale-alarm counts, and top-N nuisance rankings are computable over existing endpoints, with aggregation staying page-side per the decision-37 precedent.

What would need contract work or a recorded decision before implementation:

- A *named* managed-state axis. The standards require shelved, suppressed-by-design, and out-of-service alarms to be visibly distinct from simply absent [2 §§11.7.4, 11.8.3, 11.9.4]. Per-kind `Out` status ports (`shelved`, `out_of_service`, `suppressed`) can carry it within the seam — but a uniform cross-kind vocabulary the pane renders as managed-state lists is a convention the planner must record, and elevating managed state to a first-class served field is contract work.
- Lifecycle journal events. Shelve/unshelve, suppression transitions, and out-of-service changes currently journal only as point transitions and settled receipts; dedicated `JournalEvent` variants were rejected with decision 33, so named alarm-lifecycle events would reopen a recorded decision.
- A shelve/suppress command variant. Operator shelving through a dedicated `Command` was rejected in decision 38's alternatives; the writable-point path covers it — changing that is contract work.
- An in-model rationalization record. The master alarm database — consequence, required action, response time, priority, class per alarm [4, 13] — is today engineering data outside the model. Descriptor metadata can carry priority/class; carrying the full rationalization record inside the plant model is schema work and a decision in its own right.
- Alarm delivery and escalation. The unattended-station obligation is delivery — transmission to staffed facilities, re-alarming, escalation [11; 5 §8] — which is notification machinery the simulated transport does not have; if a client requires it in-contract rather than as deployment integration, that is a new surface, not an alarm-state extension.

## Proposed DCS implications

The accepted requirements in `docs/requirements/water-wastewater.md` now make the following direction planning authority. Detailed site policy remains customer-configured.

- Preserve the implemented two-flag acknowledgment axis as the compatible base, then add the declared rationalization metadata, managed-state visibility, and lifecycle behavior required by `WW-ALM-001` and `WW-ALM-002`. The IJmuiden scenario supplies observable evidence for `WW-ALM-003` without prescribing one client's priority names or colours [18, 19].
- If `WW-ALM-001` is promoted, land each managed state as a per-kind sibling preserving the `in`/`ack`/`alarm`/`unacknowledged` vocabulary plus managed-state `Out` ports — a shelving kind with declared maximum shelve ticks and automatic return [15], an out-of-service kind with manual-only return [1 §11.8] — so the summary pane, history, and checkpoints consume them as ordinary status-role ports and journaled writes.
- Keep designed and state-based suppression as declared wiring rather than a kind: the station's availability and mode states are the engineered suppression conditions [5 §6.3], and per-plant wiring is where suppression-by-design belongs — with the managed-state visibility caveat above recorded for the planner.
- Carry priority as descriptor/parameter metadata first [9]; promote it to a status port only if a client requires state-driven priority changes [5 §6.4] or live priority on the pane.
- Treat the plant model plus descriptors plus the durable journal as the master-alarm-database system of record — the alarm declaration, its rationalization metadata, the acknowledgment audit, and the performance-metrics source in one versioned artifact [4, 13] — rather than a separate database. Whether the model itself should embed full rationalization fields is the contract question recorded above.
- Raise alarm *delivery* — telemetry to staffed facilities, call-out escalation — as the first client question for unattended stations [11], since owner evidence points there before shelving or flood suppression.

## Assumptions needing customer validation

- Whether the first client operates an alarm philosophy conforming to ISA-18.2/IEC 62682, and which site-specific vocabulary and administrative rules it uses. This configures the accepted alarm capability; it no longer gates its place in the water foundation.
- Which alarms must latch until acknowledged — the station note's open question; the only owner datapoint is the latched high-level alarm reset [10].
- The required priority scheme: number of levels, colour and indication conventions, whether a per-alarm criticality mark like [9]'s suffices, and whether priority is static or state-driven [5 §6.4].
- If shelving is required at all: which alarms may be shelved, per-alarm maximum shelve times including non-shelvable alarms, shelving authority and reason capture, and the shift-review workflow [15, 2 §14.3] — or whether shelving is unwanted on an unattended station.
- Which alarms are state-dependent — for example whether a maintenance-inhibited pump's fail-to-start should be designed-suppressed — versus covered by availability wiring already in the station contract.
- Whether out-of-service is an alarm state the client expects, or whether the equipment-level maintenance-inhibit mode already expresses their intent.
- Flood expectations: whether the client wants alarm-rate monitoring (computable from journal and history) or engineered flood suppression [5 §6.7]; and whether stale-alarm and chattering metrics are acceptance evidence.
- Alarm delivery for unattended stations: the telemetry destination, call-out or escalation requirements, and whether a discrete communication-failure alarm is needed beyond the I/O-health surface — carried over from the station note.
