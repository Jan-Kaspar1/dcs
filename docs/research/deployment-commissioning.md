# Deployment, backup, upgrade, and commissioning requirements for the water-sector lifecycle

- **Checked:** 2026-09-15
- **Question:** What do owner specifications, standards, and regulator guidance actually require of a water-sector control system's lifecycle — redundancy and failover expectations for controllers and networks and what the operator must see of them; backup-and-restore requirements for engineered configuration and runtime state; upgrade and rollback practice for in-service control software, including whether uninterrupted download or takeover is expected and how it is witnessed or tested; the commissioning workflow an engineer performs and hands over on site; and the record-retention and audit expectations the journaled event record would feed — so that `WW-LCM-002` can carry either observable acceptance criteria or a concrete revisit condition?
- **Affected requirements:** `WW-LCM-002`; edges touch `WW-LCM-001` (controller continuity, already accepted) and `WW-SEC-001` (audit/access, still candidate)

## Supported findings

### Redundancy and failover expectations — and what the operator sees of them

- Controller and server redundancy is owner-specified at plant/utility scale rather than at the single-station scale: a municipal PLC hardware standard carries hot-backup CPU modules in its approved hardware list and dedicates sections to distributing CPUs within a plant and to control-network design [13]; a utility design standard requires documented availability/redundancy and UPS positions as design deliverables [14 §7.7–7.9]; a county SCADA standard maintains redundancy by duplicating the supervisory project and consults preferred hot-standby PLC configurations [20]; a regional water SCADA standard runs hot-standby server pairs whose standby doubles as the historic database, with redundant client connections [21]. The standards' normative backup is on the availability side: control-system backup and recovery to a known secure state are explicit requirements (SR 7.3/7.4) [5].
- What the operator must see is consistent across sources: failover does not hide the pair's health — the supervisory layer follows the active member (clients configured with redundant connections, switching to the active server [21, 26]), and transitions into degraded or backup control are annunciated events (backup-control-mode engagement alarmed as critical at the station [22]; station alarms transmitted to staffed facilities [23]; alarm presentation including identity, time, and acknowledge status specified by the owner [20]).
- Failover capability is *tested*, not merely installed: the control-systems testing standard includes an availability test inside the proof-of-performance test [15 §13.2], and the commissioning code's hold points make acceptance-to-service a signed gate before a system joins live operation [17].

### Backup and restore — engineered configuration and runtime state

- The water-sector baseline is explicit about scope and cadence: regular scheduled backups of everything needed to operate — named examples include network configurations, PLC logic, engineering drawings — stored separately from the source systems, tested regularly, layered in depth, and kept to a 3-2-1 rule [8]; the federal sector guidance treats system backups and logging as part of the cybersecurity baseline [9].
- Restore scope is an enumerated artifact set, not just "the program": program and logic files, configuration details, I/O lists, firmware, HMI graphics, license keys, vendor configuration tools, support documentation, and system images, with backup integrity mechanisms (hashing, write-once media) and backup management folded into change and risk management and exercised during recovery drills [7].
- The standards scale the requirement: backups must be producible without affecting normal plant operation, with the backup mechanism verified and validated at higher security levels and automated at the highest; recovery and reconstitution return the system to a known secure state after failure, conventionally documented as a tested step-by-step recovery procedure [5 SR 7.3–7.4; 6 §3.3.9].
- Backups are also a *handover artifact*: an owner SAT report is required to include software backups, settings and parameters, and the software and licenses needed for operation and maintenance including diagnostic software [17]; commissioning document lists cover the records precommissioning and commissioning consume [2 Annex A].

### Upgrade and rollback — in-service control software

- Lifecycle guidance treats software change as a managed program activity: applicability and risk assessment, owner qualification of a patch or update before installation, a written installation procedure, qualification and validation after installation, documented removal/rollback/restoration procedures, phased deployment scheduling, verification of installation, staff drills, and change management with outage scheduling [4 Annex B].
- Uninterrupted takeover is the mechanism owner redundancy exists to enable: the hot-standby pairs in owner standards [13, 20, 21] are the same shape the vendor's water-industry specification uses for continuous online configuration — activating changed project states during operation without interrupting process mode [26 §2.3]. The owner evidence supports expecting a *deploy without process interruption* where redundancy exists, and a witnessed, scheduled cut-over where it does not — the testing standard names a cut-over plan among its deliverables [15].
- Changes are witnessed through the same test machinery as commissioning: approved test plans before testing, witness/hold points, signed-off reports [1, 17, 24], and alarm-side changes pass through management of change [12 §17; 16].

### The commissioning workflow — configure, verify, hand over

- The phase structure is standardized: completion of erection → precommissioning (mechanical completion) → commissioning (cold then hot) → performance test → acceptance of plant, with document lists and certificate templates for the phase boundaries [2].
- I/O checkout is the loop check: point-to-point verification from the field device through the marshalling to the controller and back, defined as the work between completed loop construction and cold commissioning, applicable through the plant's whole life including after commissioning [3]; owner practice instruments it — instruments tested at 0/25/50/75/100% of span rising and falling [17], point-to-point testing as a named minimum SAT test [15 §12.2], and loop diagrams zoned I/O-point / panel / back-of-panel / field [24].
- Engineered configuration on site includes the operator data: initial configuration of operator setpoints and alarm values is a commissioning activity [17]; alarm-system commissioning requires the rationalized master alarm database with a test certificate per alarm and operator-response procedures [16; 12 §§8–9].
- Handover is document turnover with named contents: FAT/SAT reports and punch lists [1], work-as-constructed drawings, O&M manuals, spares, warranties, maintenance plans [18], calibration certificates [19], and software backups, settings, parameters, and licenses [17] — followed by a proving period, typically a minimum 30 days of continuous operation without a significant fault, restarting on fault [17], or an operational test and process-proving stage [18].

### Record retention and audit — what the event record feeds

- Regulatory retention binds the monitoring record, not the controller's event log directly: NPDES permits require monitoring information — expressly including calibration and maintenance records and original strip-chart recordings of continuous monitoring instrumentation — retained at least 3 years, 5 or more for sludge [10]; drinking-water systems retain analysis records 5–10 years, corrective-action records 3 years, and sanitary-survey material 10 years [11]. The control system's journaled and historized record is the feedstock these records are produced from.
- Audit expectations on the control system itself are the security standard's: authorized read-only access to audit logs, served automatically through programming interfaces [5 SR 6.1]; sector guidance adds logging sufficiency to the baseline [9].
- Alarm-audit expectations are lifecycle-shaped: monitoring/assessment and audit stages operate over the alarm and event record, and changes to alarms pass management of change [12 §§16–18] — the same durable event record the operator-action trail lives in.

## Existing seams versus contract gaps

What the implemented mechanisms already cover, needing no contract work:

- **The hot-standby pair itself.** Active/standby peers with per-scan checkpoint pull, the driver-boundary write gate, scan-boundary promotion/demotion preserving exactly one field writer, staged-output divergence detection as a promotion gate, heartbeat-loss self-promotion with field-side fencing, and the pair-as-one-logical-controller view (roles, convergence, unreachable-peer fault) — decisions 10–15, 19, 26, 28 — match the owner hot-standby expectation [13, 20, 21] and give the operator the visible role/transition surface the sources require [22, 23].
- **Cross-build upgrade.** `format_version`/`SUPPORTED_FORMAT_VERSIONS` plus `model_fingerprint` negotiation makes a new-build standby's compatibility a named pre-flight answer, and a failed negotiation leaves the running active untouched — the phased, verified installation shape [4 B.6–B.7] composed from existing machinery (decision 27; #66, #133).
- **In-service configuration change.** The `--revised` armed standby, the classify-then-apply carryover rule, the `CarryoverReport` naming what crossed, the `Reinitialized` sync state and journaled `Reinitialized` event, and `dcs-model diff` as the pre-roll review surface (decision 25, #87, #114) are the engineered, recorded in-service change the commissioning evidence assumes [4, 17].
- **Restart recovery.** `--state-file` persists the versioned checkpoint every completed cycle by atomic write-then-rename and resumes on existence with named failures (decision 35, #115) — the lone-controller recovery half of the restore story.
- **The durable audit record.** `--journal-file` appends every journaled entry — receipts carrying declared `actor` attribution, quality transitions, role changes, divergence and reinitialization events — across restarts with run-boundary markers (decisions 36, 39, 48; #163, #165), feeding the retention and audit expectations [10, 11; 5 SR 6.1] through the ordinary `GET /journal` surface.
- **Commissioning support machinery.** `dcs-model` `validate`/`lint`/`diff`/`schema` and `dcs-controller --check` are the engineering-side checks before a document reaches a rig (decisions 40, 46); persistent forcing with substituted quality, journaled and checkpointed (decision 21, #84), is the loop-check-substitution primitive [3, 17]; I/O-health counters and scan-overrun reporting (decision 22, #83) supply the point-health view checkout relies on; the scripted two-peer rig tests and the checked-in compose rig (decision 49) are the FAT-equivalent engineering evidence.
- **Artifact identity.** The fingerprint makes "which document runs" a negotiated fact, and decision 46 keeps documents deployment-supplied — the mounting discipline owner backup practice assumes [7, 8].

What would need contract work or a recorded decision — the gap list implementation tickets would consume:

1. **Deployment topology beyond one pair per model.** Decision 47 keeps plant topology in page URLs: there is no checked-in artifact or tooling that names a deployment's pairs, and nothing validates that the deployed pair set is the intended one. Owner practice expects a control-system network diagram and topology deliverables [14 §7.9.1]; whether that lands as deployment tooling, a checked topology document, or stays owner-procedural is a recorded-decision question.
2. **Configuration-backup artifacts.** No defined "backup set" exists: the model document, dynamics document, state file, journal file, and the running build are separate deployment-supplied paths (decision 46), and nothing enumerates the set a restore needs or proves a backup restores — the enumerated restore set and tested-restore expectation in [5 SR 7.3–7.4; 7; 8] has no counterpart. A backup manifest (model fingerprint, document set, build identity) plus a verified-restore procedure is the contract/tooling question.
3. **Rollback semantics.** The machinery can run the previous build as a standby and promote it back, but rollback is not a named direction: `format_version` negotiation is forward-tolerant only (an old build cannot accept a newer checkpoint format), and the revision carryover rule classifies crossing into the *revised* model — carrying state back to the prior revision after a failed roll is undefined (decisions 25, 27). Whether rollback means "promote the retained old build back" or "restore the pre-change backup set" [4 B.6.7] needs a recorded decision before implementation.
4. **Commissioning evidence artifacts.** FAT/SAT-style witnessed test plans, punch lists, signed-off reports, and per-alarm test certificates [1, 15, 16, 24] are external document workflows today; the scripted rig tests are engineering evidence, not acceptance records. If a client wants acceptance records generated from the platform — a scripted witnessed run bound to a model fingerprint — that is new tooling surface.
5. **Commissioning-mode policy.** Forcing exists and is journaled, but there is no declared commissioning workflow: whether outstanding forces must be enumerated, alarmed, or expired before handover — the "return forces/inhibits" discipline checkout practice implies [3, 15] — is a policy and possibly a contract question (e.g., force-set reporting as a checklist).
6. **Audit retention and integrity.** The journal file is append-only and durable but has no retention policy, export/archive mechanism, or tamper evidence; the regulatory retention periods [10, 11] sit on the operator's records system, and authenticated access stays deployment-fronted per decision 48 — a client audit-integrity specification would reopen that decision.
7. **Failover as demonstrated availability evidence.** Automatic failover is opt-in (`--auto-promote`, decision 28) and proven in scripted tests; an owner's availability test [15 §13.2] would need a demonstration artifact or accepted scripted evidence — deployment evidence, not contract work, unless a client requires the platform to produce it.
8. **Delivery of alarms to staffed facilities.** The telemetry/call-out path [23] is notification machinery outside the simulated transport — the same gap the alarm note records; lifecycle work does not close it.

## Proposed DCS implications

*All items in this section are proposals, not decided behavior.*

- Treat the redundant pair as the unit of deployment and the revision roll as the upgrade mechanism: the implemented sequence — new build or revised model starts as standby, negotiates fingerprint/format, converges or reinitializes with its carryover report, promotes at a scan boundary — is already the phased, witnessed change the sources describe [4, 15, 17]. Record the inverse as the rollback proposal: retain the old build/model as a standby through the roll so a failed promotion reverses by the same demote/promote order, and record explicitly that state carryback across the revision boundary is out of scope until decided (gap 3).
- Define the deployment backup set as a documented manifest rather than machinery: the model document plus its fingerprint, the dynamics document, the state file, the journal file, and the build/image identity — the enumerated restore set of [7, 8] applied to this platform's artifacts (decision 46's deployment-supplied paths are already the right shape). A "verified restore" then means: present the set, start the pair, and observe fingerprint negotiation and resume succeed — testable without contract change.
- Map commissioning stages onto existing seams: `--check`/`lint`/`diff` for the design-verification stage; forcing plus I/O health for loop-check support; the scripted rig for FAT-equivalent evidence; the journaled transition record for the SAT witness record. Whether the platform generates signed acceptance artifacts stays a customer question (gap 4).
- Keep the operator's view of lifecycle events on the surfaces already journaled: role transitions, divergence, and reinitialization are `JournalEvent`s the pair view renders; a deployment checklist surface (outstanding forces, unconverged peers, non-good I/O) would compose existing telemetry rather than new contract.
- Keep record retention as deployment policy: the journal file's append-only record plus a rotation/archival procedure feeds the 3–10 year retention expectations [10, 11]; authenticated access to it stays at the decision-48 seam.

## Assumptions needing customer validation

- Whether the first client's station class expects controller redundancy at all — plant-scale owner standards specify hot-standby controllers and servers [13, 20, 21], while small-station specifications emphasize telemetry delivery and a backup control mode over redundant controllers [22, 23]. This gates whether `WW-LCM-002` acceptance leans on the pair or on the lone-controller state-file path.
- The expected availability target and failover mode: manual promotion versus armed `--auto-promote`, and whether failover must be demonstrated as witnessed availability testing [15 §13.2; 17].
